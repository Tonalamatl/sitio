The question behind the model

How far back does the count go?

The tradition we follow keeps a count of 13,780 years. Counting back through it, our model places Day Zero on a winter solstice: March 16, 11,755 BCE, in the Julian calendar. Sunrise on that day marks the beginning of the age of the Fifth Sun. On that day, the Tonal and the Xiuhtonal are both 1 Cipactli, and the year begins with its first veintena, Atlakawalo.

A winter solstice in March isn’t a mistake. Over that many centuries, the Julian calendar drifts away from the Sun by about three quarters of a day every hundred years.

That solstice brought the longest night of the year. The center of the Milky Way passed directly over the spot where the Templo Mayor would stand thousands of years later. At dawn, Venus shone beside the Pleiades.

The written dates help verify the 260-day count. Starting it on that solstice is our model’s proposal.

How we arrived at that date
  1. We found the solstice. Starting from the December 2025 solstice, we went back the 13,780 years our tradition keeps. The model places that solstice on March 16, 11,755 BCE, in the Julian calendar.
  2. We counted the nights. There are 5,033,047 nights between the two solstices. The calculation accounts for the Earth spinning a little faster in the past. Over 13,780 years, that difference adds up to about seven days.
  3. We compared the two counts. When we count the Tonal back from 1 Coatl, the day Tenochtitlan fell, that solstice comes out as 1 Cipactli. Without those seven days, it would come out as 8 Toxtli.

From Day Zero to 1325

Thousands of years following the Sun

For thousands of years, the year began on the winter solstice, the shortest day. After the 18 veintenas came five Nemontemi, or six when the Sun took one more day to return. No one had to count leap years. The sky marked the day, so the year never drifted.

Ancient peoples had no clocks, but they had buildings and mountains that measured the year.

  • Teotihuacan260 and 105 days in the landscapeThe Avenue of the Dead and the Pyramid of the Sun face sunrises that split the year in two. From August 13 to April 30, there are 260 days. From April 30 to August 13, there are 105.
  • Olmec and MayaThe same dates for 1,500 yearsFrom 1100 to 750 BCE, several sites were aligned to February 11 and October 29. Those dates stayed in use for 1,500 years and moved by only one day, because they followed the Sun, not a star.
  • XochicalcoA cave that measures the yearA shaft almost nine meters (30 feet) deep lets sunlight in for 105 days, from April 29 to August 13. For the other 260 days, the cave stays dark.

Xochicalco: the year begins at the solstice

On the Temple of the Feathered Serpents, the sign Calli pulls a rope tied to the day 11 Ozomatli, next to the year 6 Acatl. Since 1945, archaeologists have read the scene as a calendar adjustment.

That 11 Ozomatli falls on December 18, 679, the day of the winter solstice. In the solstice count, it is Atlakawalo 1, the first day of the year. If the year 6 Acatl were 52 years earlier or later, in 627 or 731, 11 Ozomatli would fall thirteen days from the solstice. Only in 679 do the carving, the Sun, and the start of the year line up.

Other dates at Xochicalco match today’s Tonal to the day. The total solar eclipse of May 1, 664, was visible there, with two and a half minutes of darkness. It fell on 9 Ehécatl, the day the archaeologist Rubén Morante read on the temple’s sloping wall.

Morante read seven dates at the site, and all seven match our count.

1325 · The year of Tenochtitlan

The year that moved to a new sunrise

When México-Tenochtitlan was founded in 1325, the year stopped beginning at the solstice. From then on, it began at the end of winter, when the Sun rose behind Mount Tláloc, the most sacred mountain in the Valley of Mexico. In our count, the first year under this rule begins on February 14, 1325. Dates before 1582 are in the Julian calendar, which was then eight to ten days behind today’s calendar.

The mountain marks two dates, depending on where you watch from. In today’s calendar, seen from Tepeyac, the Sun rises behind Mount Tláloc on February 23 or 24, and that day is Atlakawalo 1. Seen from the Templo Mayor, it rises behind the mountain on March 14 or 15, and Tlakaxipewaliztli begins that day. The two dates are twenty days apart: exactly one veintena.

Near the equinox, the point where the Sun rises shifts a lot from one day to the next. On only one morning that season does the Sun rise right behind the peak. With that marker, the year doesn’t drift, because the horizon adds the leap day.

The January eclipses confirm the change. If the year begins in December, a January eclipse falls in the new year. If it begins in February, the eclipse falls in the old one. The Huichapan Codex places the solar eclipse of January 12, 1404, in the year 2 Acatl. With the year starting at the solstice, it would be 3 Tecpatl. With the year starting at Mount Tláloc, it is 2 Acatl, just as the codex says. The same is true of the eclipse of January 2, 1508.

1440: the last year in step

In 1440, a year 13 Tecpatl, Itzcóatl died and Moctezuma Ilhuicamina was chosen to rule. That year, the count was exactly in step with the Sun. Atlakawalo 1 began on February 14, with the Sun behind Mount Tláloc as seen from Tepeyac. Twenty days later, on March 5, Tlakaxipewaliztli began with the Sun behind Mount Tláloc as seen from the Templo Mayor. In today’s calendar, those dates are February 23 and March 14.

After March 5, the year was no longer set by the mountain. It was counted as a fixed 365 days, always with five Nemontemi, and March 5 became Atlakawalo 1 again. That is why the year 13 Tecpatl lasted 385 days: its first veintena was counted twice.

No codex says when people stopped watching the mountain. Every later record matches a 365-day year counted from March 5, 1440. And 1440 is the last year in which both festivals lined up with the Sun behind Mount Tláloc.

1440–1782

The year that drifts

A year of exactly 365 days is almost six hours shorter than the solar year. It gains a day on the Sun every four years, or about twenty-four days every century. After 1440, Atlakawalo 1 came earlier and earlier.

The sixteenth-century sources show exactly that. Sahagún’s informants said Atlcahualo began on February 2, and in 1565 our count puts Atlakawalo 1 on that very day. Alfonso Caso calculated that between 1521 and 1570, the Mexica count skipped twelve leap days.

“They had already seen and felt that their year was off. When the Spaniards arrived, the masters of the calendar and the philosophers wanted to meet to correct the leap-day error they had not caught.”Friar Toribio de Motolinía, Memoriales (our translation)

The passage records an admission from the calendar keepers themselves. They knew their year was drifting and wanted to fix it, but the Conquest came first. After 1521, the count passed to families and towns, with no temples and no priests who could meet. The year kept drifting for another 260 years.

Other friars and chroniclers gave different dates for the start of the Indigenous year: March 1, March 2, and February 26. Those dates came from fixed calendars the friars made for their own use, not from the count the towns kept. That is why the friars’ dates don’t agree with each other. Indigenous dates recorded next to a Christian date do match the drifting year.

Each source in its own period

Why the sources disagreed

For a long time, the sources seemed to contradict each other. A year that starts at the solstice fits Xochicalco, but not the January eclipses. Caso’s 365-day year fits the Conquest, but not the Xochicalco solstice. The contradiction goes away once you accept that the year changed its rule twice. Each source reflects the rule of its own time.

The Tonal has not shifted by a single day in more than thirteen hundred years of records, from Xochicalco to Oaxaca to today. And the years of the January eclipses of 1404 and 1508 make sense only if the year began after January. A copyist converting the Christian year would have written 3 Tecpatl, but the codices say 2 Acatl. The only mismatch is one day in Cortés’s arrival, the same one-day gap that separates the scholars’ correlations.

1777–1782

The old count ends and a new one begins

Three hundred forty years after 1440, the drifting year had slipped all the way back to the winter solstice. Between 1777 and 1782, the sky, the stone, and the count came together.

  • January 4, 1784Alzate returns to XochicalcoHe enters the observatory. The elder who guided him in 1777 has died.

The new count didn’t begin at the solstice. It began at sunrise on March 13, 1782. On that day, the old count was at 11 Mikiztli, “eleven Death.” The drifting count ended on a Death day, and at sunrise the new count began with Atlakawalo 1.

The choice makes sense for three reasons. First, sunrise was the hour when that year was due to begin. Second, it matches Mount Tláloc: the Sun rose one day away from its sunrise behind the mountain as seen from the Templo Mayor. Third, it breaks nothing. The Tonal stays the same, and the year keeps its name, 4 Toxtli. That year lasted 447 days, because the count started over at Atlakawalo 1 in March. Since then, the year has been 365 and a quarter days long.

Family memory

Why the change was lost and how it was kept

The 1782 change was never written down. The people writing about the calendar were educated criollos, Mexican-born people of Spanish descent, such as Clavijero, Veytia, Alzate, and León y Gama. They worked from old manuscripts. In 1792, Alzate wrote that “the nation survives, but its customs do not.” The only one of them who met the keepers of Xochicalco wrote that they taught him nothing.

Families kept the count. After 1521, it lived in homes rather than temples. A count kept quietly almost never ended up in court records. The rare evidence comes from Oaxaca. In 1704 and 1705, the Zapotec towns of Villa Alta handed over 99 notebooks with the 260-day count. Their owners called it “the count of the time of the ancestors.”

Twenty-eight years after the change, the War of Independence began: eleven years of uprooted towns and separated families. In the middle of the war, on March 14, 1819, the new count began a year 2 Acatl, the year of the New Fire. There was no way for people to gather and celebrate it.

Some families kept the teaching of the count as it stood after the change. That teaching uses the same Tonal as always, with a year that begins in March, each year at its own hour. The tradition is oral. It has no documents, but it keeps the count alive, day by day.

The count today

How to read the count today

Each day has two names. The Tonal is the 260-day cycle that has continued since Day Zero. It never stops, and it changes at midnight. The Xiuhtonal is the day’s name within the year, which has 18 veintenas of 20 days plus five Nemontemi. During the Nemontemi, the Xiuhtonal pauses.

The year is 365 and a quarter days long, so each year begins at a different hour. The Xiuhtonal day changes at that same hour. In 2026, it changes at 6:45 a.m.

Each year also has two names. The Tonal year always carries the year bearers Toxtli, Akatl, Tekpatl, and Kalli, and it changes at the December solstice. The year 1 Toxtli began at the 2025 solstice. The Xiuhtonal year changes in March and carries the year bearers of its era. On March 15, 2026, the era of Atl began with the year 1 Atl.

From 1325 to 1782, the Tonal year changed on Atlakawalo 1, the same day as the Xiuhtonal year. That is why the January eclipses of 1404 and 1508 fall in a year 2 Acatl in both counts.

Maya, Zapotec, and Mexica

The same count, by other names

The Tonal and the Maya Tzolk’in move in step. The Mexica count is anchored to the fall of Tenochtitlan, on the day 1 Coatl, which the Mexica recorded. The Maya count is anchored to the Long Count. We checked all 5,033,047 nights between Day Zero and the 2025 solstice with the GMT 584283 correlation. On every one of those nights, the Tonal and the Tzolk’in give the same number and sign.

What differed from one people to another was the name of the year. A 365-day year moves the sign forward five places, so only four signs can name years. These are the year bearers. Each people named its year after a different day, so each one ended up with a different set of four signs.

Those three sets also name the three eras of the Xiuhtonal year, which are part of the tradition’s teaching. Each era lasts 1,144 years, or 22 cycles of 52 years, and each era has its own four year bearers. The era of Matzatl matches the Classic Maya period, which ended around the year 900. The era of Toxtli matches the Nahua world of the Postclassic period.

One coincidence stands out. From 1440 to 1782, Atlakawalo 1 always fell on an Atl, Yowalmitzi (Ocelotl), Kiawitl, or Kuehtzpallin day, the four year bearers of the era of Atl. For 340 years, the year was already beginning on the signs of the era that began in 2026.

What we compare across cultures

We compare the number and the position of the sign in the 260-day count. For Tikal and the two Epi-Olmec pieces from Veracruz, we convert the Long Count with the GMT 584283 correlation. For the Veracruz pieces, the Maya names are shown as equivalents of the signs. A different correlation could change the result.

How the year is counted in each period

From Day Zero to 1325. Each year begins on the day of the winter solstice. After the 18 veintenas come as many Nemontemi as are left before the next solstice: five or six.

From 1325 to 1440. The year begins on the day the Sun rises behind Mount Tláloc as seen from Tepeyac, around February 23. There are five or six Nemontemi.

From 1440 to 1782. The year lasts exactly 365 days, with five Nemontemi, and it gains a day on the Sun every four years.

Since March 13, 1782. The year lasts 365 and a quarter days, with five Nemontemi. It begins in mid-March, at a different hour each year, and the Xiuhtonal day changes at that hour.

During the Nemontemi, the Xiuhtonal pauses, while the Tonal keeps advancing one day at a time.

Why we use ΔT to date Day Zero

Astronomers calculate solstices on a uniform time scale. To know which calendar day a solstice fell on, you also have to account for how fast the Earth was spinning at the time. ΔT is the difference between uniform time and time based on the Earth’s rotation.

With an estimate of ΔT, our model places the solstice about seven days earlier than a calculation without that correction. In the continuous count of the Tonal, that date is 1 Cipactli.

Day Zero falls on 1 Cipactli if ΔT was between 6.37 and 7.37 days. Stephenson, Morrison, and Hohenkerk (2016) put ΔT at 6.93 days.
